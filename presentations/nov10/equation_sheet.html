The model in one paragraph

The economy has many manufacturing plants. Each plant has a productivity s and a type: light (manual work barely matters) or manual-intensive (casting, plastics, textiles). Every year a plant hires workers to make as much profit as it can (equations 1–4), then decides whether to stay or close (5). New plants enter until entering is no longer worth it (5). Two national totals are fixed: Korean workers N and the EPS quota M (6). We solve for the long run, where all of this is steady, once under today's rule (3) and once with a permit price (4), and compare output.

1Production: what a plant makes

y = s · ( hρ + λt ℓρ )α/ρ where ℓ = k + m
Say it: “A plant's output is its productivity times a mix of core Korean workers and manual work. Manual work matters much more for some plants, and it can be done by a Korean worker or an EPS worker.”

How it builds up

Step 1 · Productivity
s = x · z

x is drawn when the plant is born and never changes: most plants get a low x, a few get a very high one (a Pareto draw). z changes from year to year, but slowly.

Step 2 · Two kinds of work
hρ + λt ℓρ

Core work h is done only by Koreans. Manual work ℓ gets weight λt: 0.019 for light plants, 1.47 for manual-intensive plants.

Step 3 · Who does manual work
ℓ = k + m

A Korean manual worker k or an EPS worker m. They are perfect substitutes: one EPS worker does exactly the work of one Korean manual worker, no more.

Step 4 · Shape
( … )α/ρ

ρ = 0.645: core and manual work are fairly easy to swap. α = 0.69 is below 1, so each extra worker adds a bit less (decreasing returns). That is what stops a plant from growing forever.

Where it shows up later. With λ this small, light plants hire almost no manual workers, so EPS has nothing to save them and no light plant uses it (slide 42). 89% of plants are light because 82% of new plants are light and manual-intensive plants close more often (their fixed cost is higher). Perfect substitutes are why, at a large plant, an extra permit replaces a Korean worker instead of making the plant bigger (slides 49 and 51).

h, not h + k. The law counts all insured Korean workers, h + k. The model counts only core Koreans h, a simplification: hiring Korean manual workers does not raise a plant's cap. Example: a plant with h = 20 has cap 30 and may hire up to 0.21 × 30 ≈ 6.4 EPS workers.

Why plants want EPS: compare equations 2 and 3
saving per EPS worker = w × ψ(1 + h)−ξ

That is the premium from equation 2. A plant uses EPS if the saving on all its EPS workers is larger than the fixed cost cm (slide 42).

4No-size-rule experiment: a price instead

cost of m = w + p, no cap, any plant with h ≥ 1
Say it: “In the experiment the size cap disappears. Every plant pays the same permit price p, and p moves until the same quota is used up.”
pPrice of one permit, the same for every plant: 0.34, about 42% of the wage. The money goes to whoever sells the permits, not to the plant

Saving per permit is now w × premium − p. Left of the dot the premium saved is bigger than the price, so the plant uses EPS (if the total also covers cm). Right of it a Korean manual worker is cheaper, so plants with more than about 28 core Koreans drop EPS. That is why EPS users get smaller on average, from 21.6 to 13.8 workers (slide 49).

★Profit: equations 1–4 together

π = y − w·h − (cost of k)·k − (cost of m)·m − cm·1[m > 0] − cf,t
Say it: “Profit is output minus what the plant pays: core Koreans, Korean manual workers with their premium, EPS workers, a fixed cost if it uses EPS at all, and a fixed cost of running the plant.”
cmFixed cost of using any EPS workers: 0.383 (recruiting, paperwork)
cf,tFixed cost of running the plant each year: 0.40 light, 2.68 manual-intensive
1[m > 0]Equals 1 if the plant uses EPS, 0 if not

Each year the plant picks h, k and m to make π as large as possible, given w and the rule (3) or the price (4). This is the “policy function” slides 44–45 show.

5Plants exit and enter

V(s) = π(s) + max{ 0, β(1 − δ) E[V(s′)] }
Exit if β(1 − δ) E[V(s′)] < 0
Entry: Σ ν V = ce
Say it: “A plant's value is this year's profit plus the better of closing, worth zero, or staying. A plant closes when its future is not worth it. New plants keep entering until the value of entering equals the entry cost, and that pins down the wage.”
VValue of a plant. E[…] is its expected value next year, given today's s
s′Next year's productivity
βDiscount factor, 0.96: how much next year counts
δChance a plant is closed by an outside shock anyway: 0.036 a year
νWhere new plants start: x from a Pareto draw with tail 3.85, z from its usual range. 17.6% of new plants are manual-intensive
ceCost of entering, 14.7. The entrant pays it before it knows its type or productivity

Why entry sets w. If w were lower, plants would earn more, entering would be worth more than ce, and more plants would come in. Only one wage makes entering exactly worth ce: 0.830 under today's rule, 0.816 in the experiment. In the code a small random shock also enters the close-or-stay choice, so closing is a probability that rises smoothly as the value falls; this only smooths the decision.

6Markets clear: the totals are fixed

Σ μ (h + k) = N
Σ μ m = M
Say it: “Add up every plant. All the Korean workers they use equal the fixed supply N, and all the EPS workers they use equal the quota M.”
μHow many plants there are of each type and productivity. Size follows from productivity. Σ adds over all of them
NKorean workers, core plus manual, set to 1
MEPS quota, set so EPS workers are 5.5% of all workers: M ≈ 0.058 (source of 5.5% being checked)

The wage is already fixed by entry (5), so Korean labor is matched to N by the number of new plants: more entrants, more plants, more Koreans hired. The quota is matched to M by θ (today) or p (experiment).

★How the six fit together

  1. Guess θ (or p).
  2. Each plant picks h, k, m to maximize profit (1–4) and decides to stay or close (5).
  3. Wage. Find the w at which entering is worth exactly ce (5).
  4. Number of plants. Scale entry until Korean workers add up to N (6).
  5. Quota. If plants use more EPS workers than M, lower θ (or raise p); if fewer, the other way. Repeat from 2.
  6. Compare plant sizes and output under today's rule and under the permit price.

Easy to mix up

  • ℓ is not half Korean, half EPS. It is any mix: k + m. The plant chooses the split.
  • The cap counts h only in the model; the law counts h + k. The 2024 survey data do not separate core and manual Koreans.
  • θ is one number for every plant. It is not a plant's own choice; it is how the quota gets shared.
  • “EPS share” has three meanings: 36.9% is the average across EPS plants; 5.5% is EPS workers in all workers; about 10% is the share of plants using EPS (slide 43).
  • Light plants never use EPS in the model: they hire almost no manual workers, and they are already at their best size. Manual-intensive plants are the users.
  • The permit chart is the experiment only. Under today's rule there is no price, only the cap.
